# 1. Types of Online Stores and Which Ones We Target

> **Summary:** A direct-to-consumer (DTC) brand makes or owns its products and sells them mainly through its own website, so it owns the contact details of its customers. That ownership matters more than the brand's size: a $30M Amazon seller is a worse prospect than a $4M Shopify brand, because the Amazon seller cannot email most of its buyers. We also exclude dropshippers and print-on-demand stores, even though they technically have an email list, because they lack the profit margin, product quality and staying power to benefit from our work.

## 1.1 Types of online business and whether we can help

| Business type | What it means | Can they email their customers? | Ascent fit | Lhotse fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct-to-consumer brand (DTC)** | Makes or owns its products; sells mainly through its own website | Yes, fully | **Strong** | **Strong** |
| **Online-born brand (DNVB)** | An online brand that controls everything from product design to delivery | Yes | **Strong** | **Strong**, if finances are healthy |
| **Subscription brand** | Most revenue is repeat deliveries, often set up through an app called ReCharge | Yes | **Strong** | **Strong** (great for reducing cancellations) |
| **Multi-channel retailer** | Has its own stores and website, and also sells through big retailers | Web buyers yes; in-store buyers partially | **Strong** (for large repair jobs) | Depends: measure only website revenue |
| **Half Amazon, half own website** | Sells a lot through both Amazon and its own site | Only their own-site buyers | Maybe | Maybe: own site must be big enough alone |
| **Wholesale brand** | Sells mainly to retailers, not directly to shoppers | Small own-site list | Only if own site makes $1M+ | Skip |
| **Marketplace-only seller** | Only sells through Amazon, Etsy or TikTok Shop | No. Amazon hides buyer emails. | **Skip** | **Skip** |
| **Dropshipper** | Has no stock; a supplier ships each order directly | Technically yes | **Skip** | **Skip** |
| **Print-on-demand / creator merch** | Products printed only when ordered (using apps like Printful) | Rarely their own list | **Skip** | **Skip** |

The word "e-commerce" covers any online sale, from Amazon down to a one-person Shopify store. Only the rows marked Strong or Maybe are our market.

A 2026 ShipBob survey found how brands rank their main sales channel:

| Main sales channel | Share of brands |
|---|---|
| Own website | 65% |
| Amazon | 20% |
| Wholesale or retail stores | 12% |

*Source: ShipBob survey, 2026.*

The same survey found that 86% of brands sell through more than one channel. For that reason, **always measure a potential client by their own website revenue only**. Amazon and wholesale sales never go through Klaviyo, so they do not count.

## 1.2 Why we skip dropshippers and print-on-demand

Email works best when customers run out of a product they love and trust the brand. Dropshipped generic products do not build that kind of loyalty. These businesses also fail our test for three further reasons.

| Reason | Why it rules them out |
|---|---|
| **Thin profit margin** | They do not have enough margin to pay for our services |
| **Short lifespan** | They often close within months, and our work depends on a domain the brand keeps for years |
| **Low-quality subscribers** | Their lists hurt both results and the case studies we show future clients |

They are easy to spot and skip. Printful and Printify are two of the top ten apps on US Shopify stores, and an app called Fourthwall is used by 7% of US Shopify stores that also have Klaviyo.

## 1.3 Quick decision checklist for qualifying a store

Work through the steps in order. Each answer either ends the check or sends the store to the next step.

| Step | Question | If no | If yes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is the brand's own website their main way to sell? | Go to Step 2 | Go to Step 3 |
| 2 | Does the website alone make $1M+ (for Ascent) or $3M+ (for Lhotse)? | **SKIP** (they sell mainly through Amazon or wholesalers) | Keep going to Step 3, but use only website revenue numbers |
| 3 | Do they make or own their products? | **SKIP** (dropshipper, print-on-demand, reseller) | Go to Step 4 |
| 4 | Do they use Shopify or Shopify Plus? | **HOLD** (check back later) | Go to Step 5 |
| 5 | Do they use Klaviyo for emails? | Omnisend: **HOLD** (never make them switch). Mailchimp: **NURTURE** (introduce our work). | Go to Step 6 |
| 6 | Is it beauty, supplements, food or pets? | **SECONDARY**: pursue only if repeat buying is visible | **QUALIFIED**: score it (Section 3) |

## 1.4 Tricky cases

| Case | How to handle |
|---|---|
| **A $25M brand with 60% of sales on Amazon** | Their website makes $10M, which qualifies for Lhotse. Use only the website number, and expect email to cover a smaller share of revenue because Amazon buyers never joined the email list. |
| **A brand with physical stores** | A great Ascent candidate: more sending tools (such as a store POS system) mean more security gaps to fix. For Lhotse, check whether in-store buyers are added to Klaviyo at checkout. |
| **A company that owns several brands** | Each brand has its own email domain and list, so each one is a separate Ascent project. Pitch the whole group, but price each brand separately. |
| **A brand where most revenue is subscriptions** | Subscriptions do not count as "email revenue" in Klaviyo reporting, so email looks weaker than it really is. Judge by cancelled-subscription prevention and cross-selling instead. |
| **A Shopify Plus store with a custom build** | Website scanning tools might not detect their apps. Confirm by signing up for their email list and reading the technical headers in the email you receive (Section 17 explains how). |

# 2. Why Ad Costs Are Killing Profit (and How Email Fixes It)

> **Summary:** Online shopping keeps growing, but the cost of getting each new customer is growing faster than the profit that customer brings on their first order. At the current rate of ad price increases, the average clothing brand's first sale will lose money within two years. Email keeps customers coming back at almost no cost, and that is what GrowMailers sells.

## 2.1 The size of the US online shopping market

| Measure | Figure | Note |
|---|---|---|
| US online shopping, Q2 2026 | $340.2 billion | US Census Bureau |
| Share of all US retail, Q2 2026 | 17.1% | US Census Bureau |
| Growth vs. the same quarter a year earlier | +12.2% | US Census Bureau |
| US online shopping, full year 2025 | About $1.23 trillion | |
| DTC brands' share of US online shopping | About 19% (roughly $234 billion) | Expected to stay about the same through 2028 |

*Source: US Census Bureau, Q2 2026.*

DTC brands are those that cut out retailers and sell directly to shoppers. There is a catch in the growth figures: some of it comes from higher prices due to tariffs and inflation, not more units sold. Brands are making more dollars, but not necessarily more profit.

## 2.2 Why ads keep getting more expensive

Meta (Facebook and Instagram) reported a 12% rise in its average ad price in mid-2026, while the number of times an ad was shown went up 14%. If conversion rates stay the same, brands spend about 12% more to get each purchase. Google advertising works the same way: it is an auction, and prices keep going up.

The table shows what happens to a clothing brand's profit per first order if ad costs keep rising at 12% a year.

| Year | Ad cost per purchase | Profit left from first order |
|---|---|---|
| Today | $39.24 | $5.62 |
| +1 year | $43.95 | $0.91 |
| +2 years | $49.22 | **−$4.36 (a loss)** |
| +3 years | $55.13 | **−$10.27 (a bigger loss)** |

*Our estimate, assuming prices and conversion rates stay the same.*

This does not cause an immediate crisis. The first order simply earns a little less money each month until it quietly starts losing money. Brands that already earn profit from repeat customers can handle this, while brands that do not will find their growth plan stops working.

## 2.3 Import taxes (tariffs) and higher costs

Import rules changed several times in 2026. The US paused duty-free entry (where small packages below $800 came in without taxes) and is ending it permanently on 1 July 2027.

| Who is affected | Impact |
|---|---|
| Brands that make products overseas | Now pay higher import taxes |
| Brands in Canada, the UK and Australia shipping to US customers | Now face duties they did not pay before |

Several DTC businesses closed in 2025 because of this combination of tariffs, inflation, higher ad costs and less investment money available.

## 2.4 The cost of getting a new customer vs. keeping one

There are two ways to measure ad spend per customer, and they give different answers.

| Measure | How it is calculated | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost per purchase** | Ad spend divided by all purchases, new and returning together | This is what Triple Whale (an analytics tool) reports |
| **Cost per new customer** | Ad spend divided by *new* customers only | Always higher than cost per purchase |

The worked example below shows how many sales a beauty brand needs to recover the cost of getting one new customer.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Cost per new customer | 1.5 × the $33.44 cost per purchase | About $50 |
| Profit per sale | After product and shipping, before ads | About $38.93 |
| Sales needed to break even | $50 ÷ $38.93 | About 1.3 sales |

| Product type | Sales needed to recover the cost of a new customer |
|---|---|
| Beauty, supplements, clothing | About 1.3 to 1.6 |
| Food, pet | 2.2 to 2.5 |

**In every product type we target, the brand needs customers to come back.**

## 2.5 Why getting customers back is so profitable

Getting customers back (called "retention") earns more money through three levers.

| Lever | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Repeat rate** | What percentage of buyers come back at all |
| **Frequency** | How often they buy |
| **Profit per repeat order** | Bundles, upsells, and not giving away unnecessary discounts |

The worked example below uses an $8M beauty brand that gets 50,000 new customers a year.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Raise the 12-month repeat rate | 25% to 30% of 50,000 new customers | 2,500 more returning customers |
| Extra repeat orders | 2,500 customers × 1.4 orders each | 3,500 orders |
| Extra profit | 3,500 orders × about $39 profit per order | Roughly **$136,000 per year** |
| Compare with Lhotse | Extra profit vs. the annual cost of Lhotse | 2 to 4 times the cost |

That is a large return from a single improvement.

## 2.6 How to talk about this with clients

| Talking point | How to use it |
|---|---|
| **Talk about profit, not email open rates** | "Orders you don't pay Facebook for" is what founders and finance people care about. |
| **Compare Lhotse to buying the same orders through ads** | At a $40 average ad cost per purchase, 150 extra repeat orders a month would cost $6,000 in ad spend. Lhotse is the same price. |
| **Do not use made-up statistics** | The popular claim that "customer acquisition costs rose 60% in five years" comes from a 2022 vendor blog post with no real data behind it. Smart buyers know this. |

# 3. How to Read a Store's Tech Setup to Judge Fit

> **Summary:** The apps a brand uses on its website tell us its size, budget and likely problems before we ever talk to it. Shopify plus Klaviyo is the minimum requirement, and paid growth tools like Attentive or Northbeam signal a larger brand with a real retention budget. We use these signals to give each prospect two scores: a **Lhotse Fit Score** (how good a Lhotse client they would be) and an **Ascent Urgency Score** (how badly their email delivery needs fixing).

## 3.1 What tools DTC brands use

| Category | Tools |
|---|---|
| **Storefront** | Shopify; Shopify Plus ($2,300–2,500/month) |
| **Email and SMS** | Klaviyo; Postscript; Attentive; Omnisend |
| **Subscriptions** | ReCharge (handles repeat delivery orders) |
| **Analytics** | Triple Whale; Northbeam (ad tracking tools) |
| **Customer service** | Gorgias (support chat and tickets); Okendo or Yotpo (reviews) |
| **Fulfillment** | 3PL warehouse partner (84% of brands use one) |

Every tool that sends emails on the brand's behalf (support, reviews, subscriptions) is another one we need to check in an Ascent audit.

## 3.2 What each tool tells us

| Tool | Share of US Shopify stores using it | What it signals | What we say in our pitch |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shopify Plus** | 2.5% | Strong signal of a bigger brand (fees alone cost $27,600+/yr, so almost always a $1M+ brand) | Good fit for both services |
| **Klaviyo** | 14.5% | Required: this is the gate | Both |
| **Attentive** | 0.58% | Large, enterprise-level brand with a big SMS budget | Ascent; Lhotse Scale |
| **Northbeam** | 0.11% | Large ad budget and a team that cares about measurement | Lead with real lift results |
| **Postscript** | 1.7% | Running SMS; has a retention budget | Lhotse Growth |
| **ReCharge** | 2.1% | Repeat-purchase model is already running | Focus on reducing cancellations and cross-selling |
| **Triple Whale** | 1.3% | Tracks all marketing spend carefully | Frame results as "lower blended marketing cost" |
| **Gorgias** | 1.0% | Has a customer service team sending from the brand domain | Check email security settings on support emails |
| **Mailchimp** | 5.8% | Rarely a serious email program | Nurture gently |
| **Omnisend alone** | 2.2% | Often a deliberate choice | Hold; never push them to switch platforms |
| **Printful / Printify / Fourthwall** | Top-10 apps | Print-on-demand | Skip |

*Source: Store Leads, 1,179,951 live US Shopify stores, September 2026.*

Three platform facts shape how we pitch:

1. **Klaviyo is growing fast.** Revenue grew 26% in mid-2026, and brands paying Klaviyo more than $50,000/year grew 36%. Bigger brands are investing heavily in email.
2. **Shopify rewrites the email sender** when a brand has no security record (DMARC) set up. The email shows up to customers as being from "shopifyemail.com" instead of the brand's domain. This is a clear sign the brand needs Ascent.
3. **Owners love Klaviyo's power but hate the bill.** GrowMailers sells the expertise to make Klaviyo worth paying for. We never push them to switch.

### Ascent Urgency Score (out of 100)

This score measures how badly the prospect's email delivery needs fixing.

| What we measure | Criteria | Points |
|---|---|---|
| **Security setting gaps** (max 40) | No DMARC record 20; two or broken DMARC records 20; emails show "via klaviyomail.com" above 5,000 subscribers 20; Shopify is rewriting their email sender 15; SPF record is broken 10 | Up to 40 |
| **How exposed they are** (max 30) | List above 50,000 10; using SMS heavily with frequent sends 10; in UK, Canada or Australia where Microsoft is big 5; Black Friday within 12 weeks 5 | Up to 30 |
| **Visible signs of problems** (max 30) | Test emails land in spam at 2+ email providers 15; order confirmation emails land in spam 10; no one-click unsubscribe button 10; welcome email arrives more than 1 hour late 5 | Up to 30 |

### Priority guide

Combine the two scores to decide what to pitch first.

| Lhotse Fit Score | Ascent Urgency: Low (under 40) | Ascent Urgency: High (40+) |
|---|---|---|
| **High (60+)** | **Lhotse first:** lead with program gaps and upside | **Ascent first, then Lhotse:** fix delivery, then pitch the retainer |
| **Low (under 60)** | **Nurture or skip:** check back in 3 months | **Ascent only:** for $1M to $3M or $20M+ brands |

## 3.4 Always verify before reaching out

Tech databases can be wrong and can miss some setups. A wrong signal leads to a wrong pitch, so confirm these points by hand before reaching out to any prospect.

| Check | How to confirm |
|---|---|
| Klaviyo is live | Sign up for their list and check the email you receive |
| Emails actually arrive | Confirm the signup email reaches your inbox |
| Growth tools are visible on the site | Look for a ReCharge membership portal or a Gorgias chat widget |

**Australia has the highest rate of Klaviyo use** among the six countries we target: 23.6% of its Shopify stores use Klaviyo, compared to 14.5% in the US. Australian and UK brands tend to be more experienced, so lead with delivery depth and measurement rather than basics.

## 4.4 Why these four product types

US Klaviyo users are more concentrated in these categories than the average Shopify store.

| Category | Share of US Klaviyo stores | Share of all US Shopify stores |
|---|---|---|
| Beauty and fitness | 12.8% | 10.9% |
| Food and drink | 8.8% | 7.1% |
| Health | 7.3% | 4.4% |

The key is that customers **run out of the product and buy again**. This repeat buying is what makes email so valuable.

| Product type | Typical time between reorders | Best automated email sequences | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Beauty and skincare** | 30–75 days | Quiz-based welcome, reorder reminder, reviews, VIP program | Returns because of shade match; heavy discounting culture |
| **Health supplements** | 30 days | Reorder reminder, subscription cancellation save, education series | Health claim regulations (FTC in US, ASA in UK, TGA in Australia) |
| **Food and drink** | 14–45 days | Back-in-stock, reorder, seasonal | Low profit margins; products expire; expensive to ship |
| **Pet supplies** | 30–45 days | Reorder, pet birthday, win-back | Competing with Chewy and Amazon Subscribe & Save |

*Reorder timing is a planning estimate. Always replace it with the brand's own average days between orders.*

Clothing is the largest category on Shopify (29.1% of US stores) and is a secondary target. It qualifies when repeat buying is visible, for example in basics, uniforms, or restockable essentials.

# 9. How to Measure Whether Email Is Working

> **Summary:** Open rates are no longer a useful measurement, because Apple automatically opens most emails whether or not a real person reads them. Judge an email program by click rate, purchase rate, revenue per email sent and, most importantly, holdout-tested lift (testing whether email actually caused the purchase or the customer would have bought anyway). Healthy brands earn 20% to 30% of their website revenue from email and texts. Below 8%, there is basically no real email program.

## 9.1 How much revenue email should drive

| Email's share of website revenue | What it means | What GrowMailers recommends |
|---|---|---|
| Under 8% | No real program | Full Lhotse build, start with automated sequences |
| 8–20% | Underdeveloped | Lhotse: fill sequence gaps and improve targeting |
| 20–30% | Healthy | Lhotse: optimize and test |
| 30–40% | Top tier | Ascent to protect it; Lhotse Scale if the team is stretched |
| Above 40% | Check the attribution settings | Often caused by a very wide attribution window, heavy discounting, or low ad spend |

**Always record which attribution window was used.** By default, Klaviyo credits any order placed within 5 days of an email open or click as an "email order." This overstates email's real effect, especially since Apple auto-opens inflate the open count.

## 9.2 Industry benchmarks by platform

| Metric | Klaviyo campaigns | Klaviyo automated sequences | Omnisend campaigns | Omnisend automated |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Click rate | 1.69% | 5.58% | 0.74% | 4.66% |
| Order rate | 0.97–1.34% | 1.62–2.17% | 0.08% | 1.49% |

*Klaviyo 2026 data; Omnisend 2025 data. Compare a brand only against its own platform's numbers.*

**Rule of thumb:** On Klaviyo, if a campaign's order rate is well below 1%, the email program has a problem.

## 9.3 The Apple problem (why open rates are broken)

Apple's Mail Privacy Protection (MPP) automatically pre-loads emails when they arrive. This registers an "open" in the email platform even if the person never reads the email. Litmus reported that Apple accounted for 62% of all tracked opens in July 2026, with MPP affecting about 55% to 60% of all opens.

| Problem | Why it happens |
|---|---|
| **Engagement targeting goes wrong** | A filter for "opened in the last 90 days" now includes people who never looked at the email. |
| **Subject line tests become unreliable** | When machines open half your emails, open rate cannot be trusted as a test metric. |
| **Inactive subscribers look active** | The sunset policy (removing inactive people) fails because Apple keeps triggering "opens" even for dead subscribers. |

**The fix:** define engagement by clicks, website visits and orders, not opens. Exclude Apple auto-opens where Klaviyo allows it. Only use opens as a sign of a technical problem at one specific email provider.

### Which metrics to trust

| Rank | Metric | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (most trustworthy) | Incremental revenue | Lift tested against a control group (the real number) |
| 2 | Revenue per email | Reach × relevance × conversion |
| 3 | Order rate | Whether the email produced actual purchases |
| 4 | Click rate | Whether a real person engaged |
| 5 | Delivery health | Bounces, spam complaints, inbox rate by provider |
| 6 (least trustworthy) | Open rate | Diagnostic only (broken by Apple auto-opens) |

## 9.4 Targets for a healthy Lhotse client

| Metric | Healthy range | When to act |
|---|---|---|
| Campaign click rate (Klaviyo) | 1.5–2%+ | Below 1% for 4 weeks in a row |
| Campaign order rate (Klaviyo) | About 1% or better | Well below 1% |
| Spam complaint rate | Below 0.05% | Above 0.05% (Klaviyo flags it); above 0.1% (Gmail's limit) means act now |

## 9.5 How to test whether email actually caused purchases (holdout tests)

A holdout test randomly holds back 10% of people from receiving a sequence, then compares how often that 10% buys against the 90% who got the emails. The difference shows how many purchases were genuinely caused by the email.

### Running a holdout test

| Step | What to do | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Add a random split at the start of an automated sequence | 90% get the emails, 10% get nothing (use 80/20 for smaller brands) |
| 2 | Measure orders from both groups | Use the same time period for both (for example, 14 days) |
| 3 | Run long enough | At least 4 weeks, and until the 10% group has at least 100 orders |
| 4 | Calculate incremental (real) orders | (Rate for emailed group − rate for control group) × number who got emails |
| 5 | Report both numbers | Platform-attributed and real incremental, side by side |

### Worked example

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Group sizes | 90% emailed, 10% control | 18,000 emailed; 2,000 control |
| Emailed group purchase rate (14 days) | 720 orders ÷ 18,000 | 4.0% |
| Control group purchase rate (14 days) | 56 orders ÷ 2,000 | 2.8% |
| Real incremental orders | 1.2 percentage points × 18,000 | **216 real orders** |
| Real incremental revenue | 216 × $68 per order | **$14,688 per month** |
| Platform-attributed revenue | 720 × $68 per order | $48,960 per month |
| Share that is real | $14,688 ÷ $48,960 | About 30% |

In this example, about 30% of the revenue Klaviyo "credits" to email is real. That is still a strong result. A client who hears the honest number from GrowMailers first will trust every number we report after that.

# 10. Automated Emails vs. One-Off Campaigns

> **Summary:** Automated email sequences (triggered by customer behavior) earn far more per email than one-off campaign blasts. Klaviyo's own data shows automated sequences generate about 41% of email revenue from only 5% of emails sent, at nearly 18 times the revenue per email. Campaigns still drive most email revenue overall. A complete email program needs both: a solid base of automated sequences, plus targeted, well-planned campaigns on top.

## 10.1 Automated sequences vs. campaign blasts

| | **Automated sequences** (triggered by behavior) | **Campaign blasts** (sent to a group at one time) |
|---|---|---|
| Share of emails sent | About 2–5% | About 95% |
| Share of email revenue | About 30–41% | About 60–70% |
| Revenue per email (Omnisend data) | $2.87 | $0.18 |
| Share of revenue from new buyers | About 48% | About 16% |
| Effect on delivery | Good (goes to people who want it) | Risky if sent to everyone |

Nearly half of automated sequence revenue comes from new buyers. The best brands send fewer campaigns to smaller, more engaged segments, and use the time saved to improve their automated sequences.

# 11. Keeping the Email List Clean

> **Summary:** Klaviyo charges based on the number of active subscribers in the list. Inactive subscribers and fake sign-ups from bots cost a brand twice: once on the Klaviyo bill, and again because sending to them hurts inbox delivery. Engagement tiers, a sunset sequence, careful removal of dead subscribers, and bot protection answer the most common complaint from brand owners: a rising Klaviyo bill with flat email revenue.

## 11.1 How Klaviyo charges

Since February 2025, Klaviyo charges based on every "active" profile (subscriber). It automatically moves the account to the next pricing tier when the limit is exceeded.

Removed (suppressed) subscribers leave the bill. However, they can no longer receive any emails or enter any sequences, so removing someone is a marketing decision, not just a billing one.

| Active subscribers | Monthly cost |
|---|---|
| 1,000 | $30 |
| 5,000 | $100 |
| 10,000 | $150 |
| 25,000 | $400 |
| 50,000 | $700 |
| 250,000 | $2,300 |

*Check Klaviyo's current prices before quoting any numbers to a client. A brand with 25,000 to 100,000 subscribers probably pays $400 to $1,400+ per month for email alone (our estimate).*

### A typical unmanaged list of 60,000 active subscribers

| Segment | Last activity | Subscribers | Share of list | Effect on the brand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Engaged** | Within 30 days | 12,000 | 20% | Earns almost all revenue |
| **Warm** | 31–90 days | 9,000 | 15% | |
| **Cool** | 91–180 days | 6,000 | 10% | |
| **Inactive** | 180+ days | 30,000 | 50% | Costs money, hurts delivery |
| **Bots / fake addresses** | Not applicable | 3,000 | 5% | Costs money, causes bounces |

One brand owner publicly described this exact situation: paying for 50,000+ subscribers while only about 15,000 engaged with anything, with flat revenue and a rising bill.

Removing the inactive and bot segments would bring this list to about 27,000 real subscribers. That cuts the email bill by roughly $300 per month, and all remaining emails go to people who actually respond.

## 11.2 How to categorize subscribers

Categories are based on clicks, website visits, and orders, **not opens**.

| Category | Definition | How we treat them |
|---|---|---|
| **Engaged** | Clicked, visited, or ordered in the last 30 days, or signed up in the last 30 days | Every campaign |
| **Warm** | Same actions, 31–90 days ago | 1–2 campaigns per week |
| **Cool** | Same actions, 91–180 days ago | Strongest monthly send; targeted win-back |
| **Inactive** | Nothing in 180+ days | Sunset sequence, then remove |
| **Protected** | Ordered in last 12 months, active subscriber, VIP, or has an open order or support ticket | Never remove automatically; keep in post-purchase and transactional emails |

### Who to remove (suppress)

- Hard bounces and spam complaints (Klaviyo does this automatically)
- People who did not re-engage after the sunset sequence
- Confirmed bots
- Disposable email addresses and malformed addresses
- People who received 3+ emails in 180 days with no click, website visit, or order

## 11.3 Sunset sequence process

| Step | Stage | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Entry trigger** | No click, visit, or order in 180 days AND no order in 12 months. Use 270 days for products with long buying cycles. |
| 2 | **Re-permission** | 1–2 emails over 7–10 days, with a one-click "yes, keep me subscribed" button |
| 3 | **If they click** | Move them back to the warm category |
| 4 | **If they do not click** | Remove them with a reason tag (e.g., "sunset_2026Q4") so the removal can be tracked and undone if needed |
| 5 | **Keep it running** | Run it as an ongoing automated sequence, not as an annual one-time purge |
| 6 | **Report** | How many were removed, how category sizes changed, and how click and order rates changed |

One manufacturer who removed unengaged subscribers and added a sunset flow publicly reported their open rate rising from 45% to 55% up to 65% to 70%. Opens are not a great metric (Section 9), but the direction confirms the point: emailing fewer, more engaged people improves every other metric.

## 11.4 Bots and list bombing

**List bombing** happens when bots flood a sign-up form with real or fake email addresses. The brand then pays to store junk contacts, and its welcome emails bounce or land in spam.

### Warning signs

- Sign-up spikes at odd hours or all from one country
- Random-string names or website URLs in the name field
- High bounce rates on the very first welcome email
- Clusters of unusual email domains

### How to protect sign-up forms

| Protection | What it does |
|---|---|
| **CAPTCHA or bot protection** | Added to every form |
| **Honeypot field** | A hidden field that only bots fill in (real people never see it) |
| **Double opt-in** | Used for high-risk sign-up sources: footer forms, countries the brand does not ship to, API sign-ups |
| **Disposable domain blocking** | Blocks common disposable email domains |
| **Sign-up alert** | Triggers when daily sign-ups reach 3 times the usual daily average |
| **Cohort removal** | Removes any subscriber cohort affected by a bot attack |

*This matches Klaviyo's own guidance.*

**Never buy an email list.** Purchased lists contain spam traps and dead addresses. One documented case bounced 12% on its very first send.

# 12. Email Security Settings Explained Simply

> **Summary:** Gmail, Yahoo, and Microsoft now require three email security settings (SPF, DKIM, and DMARC) from brands sending bulk email, and they reject email from bulk senders that do not meet these rules. Starting with a DMARC record that only monitors, without blocking anything, meets all current requirements, while moving to blocking mode step by step is best practice. **A missing DMARC record, or having two of them, is the most common and most actionable finding we make in a free audit.**

## 12.1 How the three settings work together

| Setting | Plain-English meaning | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **SPF** | The list of computers allowed to send email for the domain | Checks whether the sending computer is authorized |
| **DKIM** | A digital signature that proves the email is really from the brand | Signs the email with a private key; the signature survives when the email is forwarded |
| **DMARC** | The rule that ties SPF and DKIM together | Links both checks back to the visible "From" address and tells providers what to do with failures: monitor, send to spam, or block entirely |

When an email arrives, the email provider looks at three header lines:

```
From: hello@brand.com
Return-Path: bounces@send.brand.com
DKIM-Signature: d=send.brand.com
```

| Header line | What it is | Check that uses it | Question the check asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| `From:` | The visible sender the customer sees | DMARC | Did SPF or DKIM pass AND match this domain? |
| `Return-Path:` | The technical sender | SPF | Is this computer allowed to send for this domain? |
| `DKIM-Signature: d=` | The domain that signed the email | DKIM | Does the digital signature match the public key in DNS? |

**Alignment:** `send.brand.com` and `brand.com` share an organizational domain, so they count as aligned.

## 12.2 SPF: who is allowed to send email for this domain

An SPF record is a text record in the domain's DNS (the phone book of the internet). It lists every tool allowed to send email as that brand.

Example:

```
brand.com.  IN TXT  "v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:mail.helpdesk-vendor.example ~all"
```

| Part of the record | What it means |
|---|---|
| `brand.com.  IN TXT` | A text record published on the brand's domain |
| `v=spf1` | Version (marks this as an SPF record) |
| `include:_spf.google.com` | Allows Google Workspace to send |
| `include:mail.helpdesk-vendor.example` | Allows a helpdesk tool to send as @brand.com |
| `~all` | Soft-fail everything else |

### SPF rules

| Rule | What goes wrong if broken |
|---|---|
| **Only one SPF record per domain.** Having two makes SPF completely invalid. | SPF fails for everyone |
| **Maximum 10 DNS lookups.** Each `include:`, `a`, `mx`, etc. counts as one, including lookups inside lookups. | Permanent error, usually when an 11th tool is added |
| **SPF checks the technical sender,** not what the customer sees in "From:" | SPF can pass while the DMARC check still fails |
| **SPF breaks when emails are forwarded** | This is why DKIM is more reliable |

### SPF qualifiers (the ending of the record)

| Qualifier | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| `~all` | Soft fail | Normal |
| `-all` | Hard fail | Strict |
| `+all` | Authorizes everyone | Never use: anyone in the world can send as the brand |

Klaviyo's sending domain lives on a subdomain (like `send.brand.com`) and handles its own SPF and DKIM. Do not add Klaviyo to the main SPF record unless Klaviyo's setup screen asks for it. Shopify handles its own SPF for Shopify-sent emails.

## 12.3 DKIM: proving the email is really from you

DKIM adds a digital signature to every email. The signature header says which domain signed it (`d=`) and which key to use (`s=`). The public key lives in DNS and can be checked by anyone.

| Standard | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Key length | 2048-bit recommended; 1024-bit minimum |
| Alignment | The signing domain must be the From domain or a subdomain of it |
| Coverage | EVERY tool that sends as @brand.com needs a DKIM signature: email service (Google/Microsoft), Klaviyo, help desk, review app, subscriptions |
| Rotation | Keys managed through Klaviyo rotate automatically. Rotate self-managed keys at least yearly, keeping the old key live for 7+ days. |

## 12.4 DMARC: tying it all together and setting a policy

A basic starting DMARC record at `_dmarc.brand.com`:

```
v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@brand.com; adkim=r; aspf=r
```

| Setting | What it means | Starting recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| `v=DMARC1` | Marks this as a DMARC record | Always first |
| `p=none` | Monitor only; take no action on failing emails | Start here |
| `p=quarantine` | Send failing emails to spam | Step 3 |
| `p=reject` | Block failing emails entirely | Step 4 |
| `rua=` | Where to send reports | Always set this |
| `adkim=r` | Relaxed DKIM matching (subdomains count) | Use relaxed |
| `aspf=r` | Relaxed SPF matching (subdomains count) | Use relaxed |
| `t=y` | Testing flag (new in the 2026 standard) | Use while testing quarantine |

**The 2026 DMARC standard removed `pct` (percentage).** Do not raise a missing `pct=100` as a finding, because it is not one. Bringing it up with a technical buyer tells them we are out of date.

### The four-step path to full protection

| Step | DMARC record | How long | When to move on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Publish** | `p=none` + reports address | Day 0 | Reports are arriving |
| **1. Discover** | `p=none` | 2–4 weeks | Every tool sending email is identified |
| **2. Align** | `p=none` | 2–6 weeks | Every legitimate tool passes aligned SPF or DKIM; 98%+ passing for 2 weeks in a row |
| **3. Quarantine** | `p=quarantine` (optionally add `t=y` first) | 2–4 weeks | No legitimate failures; no reports of missing mail |
| **4. Block** | `p=reject` | Ongoing | Weekly review of reports; every new tool added goes through a change request |

### Who sends email as @brand.com on a Shopify brand

| Type of tool | Examples |
|---|---|
| Company email | Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 |
| Marketing | Klaviyo, Shopify Email |
| Store | Shopify notifications |
| Customer service | The help desk tool |
| Subscriptions | ReCharge |
| Customer programs | The reviews app, the loyalty app |
| Back office | Invoicing tools, HR tools, warehouse notifications |

The tools that break full blocking are always the ones nobody remembers exist.

### Why Shopify brands specifically are at risk

Shopify rewrites the sender address to shopifyemail.com when a brand has either *no* DMARC record *or two of them*. This is visible from outside and is a clean, easy-to-demonstrate finding for our free audit.

## 12.5 What email providers now require

| Requirement | Gmail | Yahoo / AOL | Microsoft |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minimum volume for bulk rules to apply | About 5,000+ emails/day to Gmail addresses; permanent once reached | Bulk senders (no published number) | 5,000+/day |
| SPF + DKIM | Required | Required | Required |
| DMARC | Required; monitoring-only (`p=none`) is enough | Required | At least monitoring-only |
| One-click unsubscribe button | Required for marketing emails | Required; honor within 2 days | Expected |
| Spam rate | Below 0.1%; never above 0.3% | Below 0.3% | Low complaints expected |
| Enforcement | Rejects some failing emails (since November 2025). Above 0.3% spam rate, Gmail stops helping until the rate is below 0.3% for 7 straight days. | Rejects or filters | Rejects with specific error code (since May 2025) |

**Every brand we target qualifies as a bulk sender.** Gmail holds 54.4% of US inboxes, so one campaign to about 9,200 US subscribers probably crosses Gmail's bulk threshold.

**Rejected email shows up as bounces in Klaviyo, not in a test inbox screenshot.** Always read the bounce reason codes.

## 12.6 Klaviyo's sending setup

New Klaviyo accounts start on a shared sending domain with shared reputation. Gmail shows these emails as "via klaviyomail.com", meaning the email looks like it came from a Klaviyo computer, not the brand's own domain. Klaviyo requires bulk senders to set up a branded sending domain.

Klaviyo's own warning thresholds are stricter than Gmail's:

| Metric | Klaviyo warning threshold | Gmail limit |
|---|---|---|
| Spam complaint rate | Above 0.05% | 0.1% |
| Bounce rate | Above 2% | No equivalent listed |

**Blocks on Klaviyo's shared sending pool are Klaviyo's problem to fix.** Every Ascent contract must say this clearly so there are no misunderstandings.

## 12.7 What is NOT a real finding

Do not use these as a reason to pitch Ascent. They are not real problems.

| False finding | Why it is not a problem |
|---|---|
| "DMARC is only p=none" | Monitoring-only DMARC satisfies all three major providers |
| "You're missing pct=100" | That tag was removed from the DMARC standard in 2026 |
| "No BIMI is hurting delivery" | BIMI adds a logo in Gmail; it has zero effect on whether emails reach the inbox |
| "Promotions tab = deliverability problem" | Gmail treats the Promotions tab as part of the inbox |
| "One seed test shows spam, so you're blocked" | Seed test accounts have no email history, so providers treat them differently |

**Lead with real findings instead:**

- No DMARC record at all
- Two DMARC records
- Failed alignment
- Shared Klaviyo domain on a big list
- Shopify sender rewrite
- SPF over its lookup limit
- Missing one-click unsubscribe
- Order confirmation emails landing in spam

# 13. How to Test Whether Emails Actually Reach the Inbox

> **Summary:** A "seed test" (sending a test email to a panel of fake test accounts) shows how an email provider treats a stranger with no history, while Google Postmaster Tools shows how the provider actually treats the brand's real customers. Sender reputation is protected by only emailing people who engaged recently, measured with real data, and dedicated sending IP addresses are only for very large, consistent senders. The industry average inbox rate is already above 80%, so "80% inbox rate" is too weak a success bar for Ascent. We use a full scorecard instead.

## 13.1 Where emails end up (industry data)

According to Validity's 2025 benchmark report, 83.5% of marketing emails reached the inbox, so about 1 in 6 missed. Spam placement rose from 4.5% in Q1 to 8.6% in Q4.

| Country | Gmail share (inbox rate) | Microsoft share (inbox rate) | Yahoo share (inbox rate) | Overall inbox rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| United States | 54.4% (88.8%) | 10.8% (75.8%) | 21.8% (86.4%) | 85.0% |
| Canada | 45.5% (94.4%) | 27.9% (78.9%) | 12.2% (88.6%) | 86.7% |
| United Kingdom | 41.1% (91.8%) | 32.3% (86.9%) | 7.8% (90.2%) | 87.8% |
| Australia | 47.3% (92.6%) | 30.2% (77.7%) | 7.5% (86.4%) | 85.0% |

*Source: Validity. "Share" is the provider's share of inboxes in that country. No data for Ireland or New Zealand.*

**Microsoft is the weak spot outside the US.** It holds about a third of inboxes in Canada, the UK, and Australia. Brands have reported months of emails going to Hotmail's spam folder even when they were only emailing 30-day-engaged subscribers, so for non-US brands we check Microsoft first.

**Gmail's "Promotions" tab is not spam.** Klaviyo calls it "a type of inbox," and no sender can choose which tab a message lands in. Since September 2025, Gmail has sorted the Promotions tab by relevance, so low-engagement senders sink within it.

The honest pitch is about staying visible inside Promotions. That is an engagement argument, not a claim to "get you out of Promotions."

## 13.2 The measurement toolkit

| Tool | What it shows | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Google Postmaster Tools | Gmail spam rate, compliance status | Needs enough daily Gmail volume to show data |
| Yahoo Sender Hub | Yahoo sender data and complaints | Yahoo only |
| Microsoft SNDS | Outlook-level data by IP address | Only useful with a dedicated sending IP |
| Klaviyo reports | Bounces, complaints, engagement by provider | Opens distorted by Apple auto-opens |
| DMARC aggregate reports | Every source sending as the brand's domain | Needs a report processing tool at larger volumes |
| Seed tests | Where a test email lands across a panel of fake accounts | No engagement history; panel mix skews the score |

## 13.3 How to use seed tests correctly

Seed test accounts do not behave like real people, because email providers treat new accounts differently. The mix of accounts in the panel also moves the score.

| Seed accounts at | Typical inbox placement |
|---|---|
| Microsoft and Apple | Around 75% to 76% |
| Gmail and Yahoo | 86% to 87% |

The right way to use a seed test:

- Weight the panel to match the brand's actual mix of subscribers (how many Gmail, how many Microsoft, etc.)
- Read the results as a trend across several sends, not a one-time score
- Always pair seed test results with Google Postmaster spam rate data, bounce reasons, and DMARC data

The real warning signs are spam placement at multiple providers at the same time, or order confirmation emails landing in spam.

### The Ascent Health Scorecard

This scorecard replaces the simple "80% inbox rate" pass bar.

| Metric | Target at 30 days | Where to check |
|---|---|---|
| DMARC pass rate on real emails | 98%+ | DMARC reports |
| Gmail spam rate | Below 0.1% (aim for below 0.05%) | Google Postmaster Tools |
| Bounce rate | Below 1% | Klaviyo |
| Microsoft inbox rate | Measurably better than before Ascent | Weighted seed test trend + provider engagement data |
| Order confirmation emails | Landing in inbox at all major providers | Seed tests and real order tests |
| One-click unsubscribe | Present on all marketing emails | Check email headers |
| Revenue per email sent | Flat or better vs. the same weeks last year | Klaviyo |

## 13.4 Shared IP vs. dedicated sending IP

| Factor | Shared IP (Klaviyo default) | Dedicated IP |
|---|---|---|
| Reputation | Shared with other Klaviyo senders | The brand's alone |
| Volume required | None | High and consistent; gaps in sending let it go cold |
| Cost | Included in Klaviyo | Extra fee plus warm-up effort |
| Main risk | If another brand on the same pool gets blocked, the brand might be affected | Every mistake is purely the brand's own problem |
| Best for | Almost every brand we target | A3 brands with large, consistent daily sending volume |

At Gmail, **domain reputation** now carries most of the weight, not the IP address. For almost every brand we target, authenticated email and an engaged list matter far more than having its own IP address.

Shared sending pools do fail. One merchant reported Gmail blocking Klaviyo's shared IPs, bouncing 22.5% and 60% of two Black Friday 2025 campaigns. Even then, the fix was escalating to Klaviyo and tightening targeting, not switching to a dedicated IP.

# 16. Email Marketing Laws by Country

> **Summary:** Cold email to businesses is easiest in the US, which runs an opt-out system (brands can email first, and recipients unsubscribe if they do not want it). It is more restricted in the UK (only companies registered with the government are fully safe) and tightly regulated in Canada and Ireland (where only specific published addresses work), while Australia and New Zealand use consent-based rules with some automatic exemptions for business addresses. Cold text messages are banned everywhere. In every country, GrowMailers must identify itself honestly, include a mailing address and a working way to unsubscribe, record where each contact came from, and never buy lists.

*This section is research, not legal advice. Confirm each country's rules with a lawyer before scaling outreach there.*

## 16.1 Rules that apply everywhere

| # | Rule | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Be honest about who you are** | A named GrowMailers sender, the real company name and accurate email headers |
| 2 | **Honest subject lines** | Subject matches the content; never use "Re:" or "Fwd:" in a first message |
| 3 | **Mailing address and working unsubscribe** | Both included in every commercial email |
| 4 | **Fast, permanent opt-outs** | Stored on one shared list used by every tool and team member |
| 5 | **Record where the contact came from** | The URL and date, plus a screenshot for Canada, Australia and NZ |
| 6 | **No bought lists** | Also no guessed or generated email addresses in strict countries |
| 7 | **No cold text messages** | Also no automated calls, AI voices or ringless voicemail, anywhere |

## 16.2 United States: the basic rules

**US federal law (CAN-SPAM)** covers all commercial email, including business-to-business. It runs on an opt-out basis, so prior consent is not required. Every message still needs the following.

| Requirement | What this means for GrowMailers outreach |
|---|---|
| Accurate email headers; honest subject line | The From, Reply-To and routing identify GrowMailers; the subject matches the email content |
| Identify the message as commercial | Say clearly that the message is about GrowMailers' services |
| Valid physical mailing address | A street address, a registered PO box or a registered virtual mailbox |
| Clear unsubscribe link, honored within 10 business days | Must work for at least 30 days after sending; no fee or login required; never sell or share opted-out addresses |
| GrowMailers is still responsible | Even if a third-party lead-gen vendor sends on our behalf |
| No harvesting addresses | Scraping addresses from sites that forbid it, or generating/guessing them, is a serious violation |
| Penalties | More than $50,000 per non-compliant email |

### US rules for calls and texts (TCPA)

| Topic | Rule |
|---|---|
| Auto-dialed or recorded/AI-voice calls and texts to mobiles | Require prior consent (texts count as calls under this law) |
| Marketing texts | Require *written* consent |
| National Do Not Call Registry | Protects residential and cell numbers |
| Business landlines | Generally exempt; but many founders use a cell phone as the business line, so scrub mobile numbers against the registry and call manually |
| Calling hours | **8 AM to 9 PM in the recipient's local time zone** |
| Fines | **$500 per violation, up to $1,500 if intentional**; class action lawsuits are common |

## 16.3 United Kingdom

| Contact type or topic | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Registered companies** (Ltd, LLP, PLC, Scottish partnerships, government bodies) | May receive B2B marketing email without consent, as long as GrowMailers identifies itself and offers an easy unsubscribe |
| **Sole traders and some partnerships** | Need **consent**. Check the UK companies register (Companies House) first; if no company is registered, do not cold email |

## 16.4 Canada: strictest rules in our markets

Canada's anti-spam law (CASL) applies to messages **sent into Canada from anywhere in the world**, including Nepal.

| Topic | Rule |
|---|---|

## 17.3 Step 2: The 15-minute outside audit (no login needed)

> **Boundaries:** Use only publicly available DNS records and emails received as a regular subscriber. No login attempts, no scanning tools, no spoofing tests, and no contact with the brand's customers.

### Part 1: Check the DNS records

| Check | What it looks up | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| 1. SPF | The domain's SPF record | Exactly ONE record |
| 2. DMARC | The `_dmarc` record | Exactly ONE `v=DMARC1` record |
| 3. MX | The domain's mail records | Shows which email provider they use |
| 4. Sending subdomain | The subdomain from the Return-Path in their email | Shows the sending setup |
| 5. DKIM | The DKIM key named in their email's headers | A valid key is published |

Run these commands in a terminal, replacing "brand.com" with the real domain:

```bash
DOMAIN=brand.com
dig +short TXT $DOMAIN | grep -i "v=spf1"             # 1. SPF: expect exactly ONE record
dig +short TXT _dmarc.$DOMAIN                          # 2. DMARC: expect exactly ONE v=DMARC1 record
dig +short MX $DOMAIN                                  # 3. What email provider they use
dig +short TXT send.$DOMAIN                            # 4. Sending subdomain (from Return-Path in their email)
dig +short TXT <selector>._domainkey.<d-domain>        # 5. DKIM key (from their email's headers)
```

Count the SPF lookups manually or with a free online SPF checker. The limit is 10.

### Part 2: Check the inbox experience

## 17.6 Step 5: Build a financial model on the discovery call

### Discovery call questions

1. "What's your email's share of website revenue, and what attribution window does Klaviyo use?"
2. "How many total orders a month, and what's your average order value?"
3. "What percentage of buyers come back within 12 months?"
4. "What's your approximate gross margin?"
5. "What's your biggest email concern right now: delivery, revenue, or both?"

### The breakeven model

Build the prospect's model live during the call using two formulas:

- **Repeat order contribution** = order value × gross margin − shipping
- **Lhotse breakeven** = monthly fee ($3,000 to $5,500) ÷ repeat order contribution. The result is the number of extra orders per month Lhotse needs to generate.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs | $3,000/month fee; $69 supplement; 70% margin; $8 shipping | |
| Repeat order contribution | $69 × 0.70 − $8 | $40.30 |
| Breakeven | $3,000 ÷ $40.30 | 74 extra orders/month |
| Share of total orders | 74 ÷ 4,000 orders/month | 1.9% needs to be incremental |

When the prospect's own model shows that only 1% to 3% of orders need to be incremental, the price objection typically disappears.
